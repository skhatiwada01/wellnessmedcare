Public-health guidance says most healthy adults can build up activity gradually without a medical visit first - but this page is not that clearance, the list below is not exhaustive, and the people most likely to benefit from moving more are often the ones with the most reason to ask first. When in doubt, asking costs one appointment.

This page is about the asking. What you should actually do is a conversation with someone who can examine you.

## When it is worth checking first

Talk to a clinician before starting or significantly increasing activity if any of these apply:

- You have a heart condition, or have had a cardiac event or procedure
- You get chest pain, pressure, or unusual breathlessness with exertion
- You have had a fall in the last year, or you feel unsteady
- You have diabetes, particularly if you take insulin or medications that can lower blood sugar
- You have joint problems, or a recent injury or surgery
- You have a lung condition
- You are pregnant or recently gave birth
- You have been largely inactive and you are over about 60
- You take medications affecting heart rate or blood pressure

This is not a list of reasons not to exercise. Almost everyone on it will be told to move more. It is a list of reasons to let the conversation shape the plan.

## What to ask

**About safety**

- Is there any activity I should avoid given my history?
- Are there symptoms during exercise that mean I should stop and call you?
- Do any of my medications change how I should approach this - timing, hydration, heart rate?

**About starting**

- What is a sensible starting point for someone at my level?
- How quickly is it reasonable to increase?
- What does "too much" look like for me specifically?

**If you have diabetes**

- How should I handle blood sugar around activity?
- Should I check before and after while I am getting used to it?
- What do you want me to do if a reading looks unexpected?

**About support**

- Would physical therapy be appropriate to get started safely?
- Is there a supervised program my insurance would cover?
- Should I be referred for a falls assessment?

## A note for older adults

If you are helping a parent become more active, or you are doing this yourself later in life, one question is worth more than the rest: **is a falls risk assessment appropriate?**

Falls are the outcome that most often turns an attempt to get healthier into a serious setback, and they are substantially preventable. A proper assessment looks at balance, strength, vision, footwear, medications that cause dizziness, and hazards at home. It is a routine thing to ask for and it is frequently not offered unless someone asks.

Balance-focused activity is often recommended for older adults specifically. What form it should take - and whether a class, a program, or physical therapy is the right starting point - is a decision for a clinician who can assess the person, not for a website.

## Getting a referral

If you want structured help rather than a general instruction, ask directly:

> "Would physical therapy or a supervised exercise program be appropriate for me? Is it covered by my plan?"

Referrals are frequently available and rarely volunteered. Our guide on [in-network care](/guides/in-network-vs-out-of-network/) covers checking coverage before you book, and the [appointment notes sheet](/tools/trackers/) is a place to write down what was agreed.

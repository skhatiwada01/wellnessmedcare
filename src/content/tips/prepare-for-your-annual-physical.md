Most annual physicals run fifteen to twenty minutes. That is not much time, and a lot of it disappears into the parts nobody controls - checking in, vitals, the computer. What is left is yours, and it goes much further if you arrive with it planned.

## Two weeks before

**Get your records in one place.** If you saw a specialist, went to urgent care, or had tests done elsewhere this year, those results may not be in your primary care record. Ask for them to be sent, and confirm they arrived rather than assuming.

**Write down your medication list.** Everything, including over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, and herbal supplements - they interact with prescriptions and they are the thing patients most often forget to mention. Our [medication list tracker](/tools/trackers/) is a blank sheet for this.

**Ask whether you need to fast.** If bloodwork is planned, some tests need it and some do not. Ask when you book, not on the morning.

## The week before

**Write your list, and rank it.** Three items, in order of what matters most to you. Not because you cannot raise more, but because if you only get through two, you want them to be the right two.

Be specific. "I have been tired" is hard to work with. "I have been falling asleep at my desk most afternoons since about March, and it has not improved with more sleep" is a starting point.

**Track anything you are going to describe.** A symptom you experience twice a week is much easier to discuss with two weeks of dated notes than with a recollection. A [symptom diary](/tools/trackers/) takes a minute a day.

## The day of

**Bring:** your medication list, your question list, your insurance card and ID, and the name and contact details of any other clinician involved in your care.

**Say your main concern in the first two minutes.** This is the single most effective thing you can do. Concerns raised at the end - the "one more thing" as the clinician reaches for the door - get the least attention, and that is often when people raise the thing that worried them most. Lead with it instead.

**Ask about screening explicitly.** Which screenings are recommended for someone your age with your history, which are due, and which you can skip. This is a conversation worth having deliberately rather than assuming it happened.

## Before you leave the room

Three questions that prevent most of the confusion that follows an appointment:

1. **What is the next step, and who is responsible for it?** Is someone ordering a test, sending a referral, or waiting for you to do something?
2. **How will I get my results, and by when?** Portal, phone, letter, and what date to expect them.
3. **What should I do if I do not hear back?** Results falling through the cracks is a real and common failure. Knowing when to chase is the fix.

Write the answers down while you are still in the room. Our [appointment notes sheet](/tools/trackers/) has a column for exactly this.

## After

Check the visit summary in your portal within a few days. It is not unusual to find something recorded differently from how you remember it, or a referral that was discussed but never placed. Catching that in week one is far easier than in month three.

If bloodwork was done, the results will often appear in the portal before anyone has discussed them with you. That is normal and it is not a signal of anything. Our [lab test reference](/understand/) explains what the individual tests measure while you wait for the conversation.
